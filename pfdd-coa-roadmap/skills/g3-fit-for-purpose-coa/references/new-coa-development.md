# Developing a new COA (G3 §III.C.2.c, §IV.E; Cano & Hobart 2011; Powers 2017; G2)

## FDA's general principles (G3 §III.C.2.c)
- Patients/caregivers and clinical experts as collaborators.
- Submit qualitative protocols, interview guides, observation checklists to FDA **before** fieldwork.
- Document every step: **item/task tracking matrix** (history of every item's creation and change),
  interview transcripts.
- Submit the psychometric analysis plan before analysis.
- Samples reflect the target population's clinical and demographic characteristics.
- Anticipate interference from processes outside the COI (component F).
- User manual; training for investigators, staff, patients, observers, clinicians.
- Scoring documented, including missing items/tasks.
- **Don't evaluate a new or significantly modified COA for the first time in a registration
  trial** — use early-phase trials (prospectively planned analyses) or a standalone observational
  study; precompetitive collaboration encouraged.

## Construct theory before items (Cano & Hobart 2011)
The central problem in health measurement: scale **content** — items grouped statistically or
thematically — often ends up defining what a scale measures, instead of an explicit **construct
theory** defining the content. Consequences: validity evidence becomes circumstantial (content/face
"validity by assumption"; correlations that show relatedness, not what is measured).

Build "bottom-up" from a construct definition:
1. **Define the COI as a continuum** — what it means to move up and down it, in patients' terms
   (from G2 concepts). Write the low end, high end, and 3–5 qualitatively distinct levels between.
2. **Predict the item ordering** — for each candidate item/task, state where it should sit on the
   continuum (e.g., "getting up from a chair" < "walking across a room" < "walking several blocks"
   < "jogging"). Name the item characteristics that should explain that ordering (task demand,
   frequency, intensity).
3. **Write items to span every level**, including both extremes (avoid floor/ceiling in the COU's
   severity range; Walton COU note).
4. **Test the theory**: after data collection, empirical item locations (Rasch/IRT) should reproduce
   the predicted ordering; misfit or reversals are findings that revise the construct theory or the
   items, not nuisances. (Stenner's "construct specification" — item characteristics explaining item
   difficulty — is the strong form.)
5. The ordered item map later becomes the backbone of **meaningful score regions** in G4 (IRT item
   parameters locate what patients at each score can do — G4 §III.B.2).

Design-time stress test (synthetic, clearly labelled): write short vignettes of patients at each
level, have the `blind-coder` agent place them using only the level definitions, and check
adjacent-level confusion. This checks the *definitions* are operationally distinct; it is not
evidence about real patients.

## Item generation and qualitative testing
- Items in patients' own words from concept elicitation; one concept per item; appropriate recall
  period (short enough to limit recall error, justified for the COU — G3 §IV.F.2); response options
  non-overlapping and reflecting real gradations; avoid VAS (G3 §IV.E.1).
- **Cognitive interviews** (Willis; Patrick 2011b): document the intended meaning of every item,
  response option and instruction; compare respondents' understanding to it; include the range of
  literacy and numeracy (component D). PerfO: cognitive interviews on instructions + pilot tasks.
- ClinRO content validity comes from clinicians like those who will rate in the trial, until
  saturation; consensus via Delphi/nominal group (Powers GMP 4, Table 4).

## Measurement model and scoring (G3 §IV.E; Cano & Hobart)
- **Reflective indicator model:** items are caused by one COI (e.g., mobility items). Specify the
  framework — CTT, IRT, Rasch — and the psychometric model (true-score, partial credit, graded
  response, polytomous Rasch); provide evidence for assumptions and fit, and dimensionality for
  multi-domain measures (e.g., CFA).
- **Composite indicator model:** items together *define* the COI (basic ADLs); items need not
  correlate. Justify item selection (e.g., patient consensus) and weights (equal-sum needs evidence
  that items are equally important).
- **Causal indicator model:** rare in regulatory COAs.
- Framework choice (Cano & Hobart): CTT sums are ordinal, sample- and scale-dependent, group-level;
  Rasch treats the model as the requirement data must meet (invariant comparison, linear measures,
  item-free person estimates, individual-level measurement, basis of item banking/CAT); IRT treats
  the model as a description of data and adds parameters to fit. FDA endorses no particular
  approach (G3 §IV.E.2) — choose, state why, and show fit.
- **Missing items** (G3 §IV.E.3): state the minimum responses to score; justify (e.g., simulation);
  collect reasons for missingness; don't mean-impute across a difficulty hierarchy (little
  difficulty walking across a bedroom ≠ little difficulty jogging).

## Before the registration trial
Reliability (G3 §IV.G): test–retest ICC in clinically stable patients with defined stability
criteria; inter/intra-rater for ObsRO/ClinRO/rated PerfO; IRT information/SE and internal
consistency are supplementary, not sufficient alone. Convergent and known-groups evidence for
component H. Interpretability groundwork for G4 (anchors: PGIS/PGIC collected alongside).
